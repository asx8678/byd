Try the three views

  • Overview shows the whole district, north up.
  • Drive follows the car with the map turned so you always drive up. Move the speed slider and watch the zoom.
  • Park makes the car look for the next gap that fits. It stops with the rear bumpers level and shows the plan: three moments to steer, the swing of the front corner, and the gap measured in metres.

What the traffic does

Every car follows a standard traffic model: a wanted speed, a time gap to the car ahead, its own acceleration and braking. Cars stop at red, decide at amber, and queue. Zoom in close to see each driver's type and speed. Stay in Park for a while and the car behind you starts to honk.

Two modes

Drive mode for the streets, Park mode to learn

On the street the car should feel like a car at 50 km/h: it leans on its tyres, runs wide if you turn too hard, and needs distance to stop. When you slow down next to a space, a detailed Park mode takes over, with exact low-speed geometry, more controls and the coach. You never press a mode button unless you want to. The car's position, speed and wheel angle carry straight over.

How the physics hands over

Vehicle simulations commonly use two models. The exact rolling model is right at walking pace. A tyre-slip model is right at speed but breaks down as the car comes to a stop. So the game blends them between about 7 and 18 km/h. Below that you get today's model, which the 128 replay checks protect. Above it you get the slip model. There's no visible jump.

Each car's mass, weight split, power, torque and tyre size come from its spec sheet. Tyre grip isn't published, so it's estimated and tuned until the car matches figures we can check: its 0–100 km/h time, its turning circle, and a braking distance in the normal range.

What Park mode adds for learning

  • Coach card: the next step and how far away it is, for example "full lock away from the kerb in 0.40 m".
  • Learning layers you switch on: the pivot point and turning circles, swept paths of all four corners, the ideal path as a ghost, the cue marks.
  • Kerb close-up: a zoomed box at the rear wheel, like a mirror tilted down.
  • Rewind 5 s and try a step again; slow motion for the first tries.
  • Numbers: wheel angle, angle to the space, centimetres to the kerb and to each neighbour.

Cars

Cars that each teach something

Each car is a data file: outline, wheelbase, overhangs, turning circle, width with mirrors, sensors and camera, and how fast it accelerates. The game already works out the steering lock from the turning circle, so a new car is mostly numbers from its spec sheet. The aim is a short list where every car is different, not 200 hatchbacks. Every plan view below is drawn to the same scale, with your Atto 2 dashed in yellow behind it.

What makes cars feel different

  • Wheelbase and steering lock decide the turning circle. The Ram turns its wheels about as far as the Atto 2, yet needs a third more room.
  • Overhangs decide how far the corners swing. A long rear overhang (911, Octavia, bus) swings out when you pull away from a kerb.
  • Width with mirrors decides whether you fit.
  • Rear-axle steering (S-Class, Hummer) changes the moves you make, and the Hummer can even drive diagonally.
  • Driver aids: sensors, camera, or nothing at all (W124).
  • Height: low fronts scrape kerbs; tall vans meet low ceilings.
  • Power and weight matter in Drive mode: the van and the truck are slow and heavy.

In Drive mode the map always shows at least the distance you need to stop, plus your car:

So it feels calm

  • It zooms out fast when you speed up (about 0.6 s) and zooms in slowly when you slow down (about 2.5 s), so it never pumps.
  • Stopping at a red light or in a queue does not zoom in. You still need to see the junction.
  • Park mode frames your car and the space together, then stops turning.
  • Pinch to zoom yourself, double-tap to give control back.
  • A truck uses its own length in the rule, so its view is wider.
  • Detail follows the zoom. Detail 0 (below 2.6 pixels per metre) shows blocks, roads and signals. Detail 1 adds lane lines, bays and car outlines. Detail 2 (above 7.8) adds wheels, mirrors, labels and dimensions. Each level fades in.

Traffic

Traffic, rules and rude drivers

AI cars use the same physics as yours: the same bicycle model and their real turning circles. A bus swings wide at a corner and a limousine can't make a tight turn, so you have to see it coming. Speed and following distance come from the Intelligent Driver Model, a standard model from traffic research. Each personality is a set of numbers plus a few behaviours, and a seed makes the traffic repeat exactly, so a level is fair and can be replayed.

Waiting for a leaver

  1. A parked car's indicator starts blinking.
  2. Stop a little behind it, leaving room for it to pull out, and signal towards the space.
  3. Cars behind pass you when the other lane is clear. If it isn't, they wait, and after a while they honk.
  4. When it has gone, the space is yours. Then park as usual.

The spot thief

  1. Thieves cruise slowly near free spaces.
  2. To reverse into a space you have to drive past it first. Signal, stop in position and start reversing within the thief's patience: 8 s on easy levels, 3 s on hard ones.
  3. Hesitate and it pulls in nose-first. You lose the space, not points, and go find another.
  4. On hard levels two thieves compete, and they also go for a leaver's space.

In Germany the space belongs to whoever reached it first, even while they pull past to reverse in, so a thief there breaks the law. In the UK it is only rude. See the tips in Lessons.

What the score checks

Red lights and stop lines. Speeding. Signalling before you pull in, pull out or turn. Blocking a junction. Parking where it isn't allowed: crossings, the zone around a junction, bus stops, loading bays, driveways, disabled bays. Touching anything. How long you held up traffic. Hazard lights used where the rules don't allow it.

Hazard lights and who gets a space depend on the country, so each map uses a country rule set. The details and sources are in the Lessons tips.

What the compiler does, in order

  1. Geometry: kerbs, blocks, lanes, buildings.
  2. Markings: lane lines, crossings, stop lines, bays.
  3. Slots: every legal place to park, with its rule (parallel, nose-in, reverse-in).
  4. Fill: parked cars, from the seed.
  5. Lanes: the route graph for traffic, with stop lines and signals.
  6. Checks: every guaranteed space is reachable, and a route planner can park the chosen car in it. A map that fails is never shipped.

Out come collision shapes, drawing layers, the lane graph and the targets. The game core never sees the spec, only this output, which keeps the Swift port simple.

A generator, not a list of levels

Same seed, same map. A level is a spec plus a seed, so "Harbour 4821" is the same street for everyone.

Difficulty knobs: spare length in free spaces (from 1.5 car lengths, the beginner's size, down to car length + 0.8 m on the hardest levels), how full the street is, lane width, how sloppily others parked, traffic density, driver mix, time limit.

The 100 free maps are hand-picked seeds plus the hand-made lessons and your garage.

Driving on the left is one switch: lanes, signals and parking sides flip for the UK, Ireland, Japan or Australia.

Drawing

Technical, with a little retro

Visual language

  • Hairlines stay 1 pixel at every zoom. Road markings are sized in metres, so they grow as you zoom in.
  • Hatching stays fixed to the screen, like a printed plan.
  • A dot grid every 10 m, like engineering paper.
  • Labels in a mono typeface, always upright when the map turns.
  • Dimension lines with ticks for gaps and clearances.
  • No gradients, shadows or textures.

The retro part is only the dot grid, the plotter-style lines and the mono labels. Everything else stays plain.

One colour per meaning

  • White: road markings, your car's outline.
  • Yellow: bay lines, your nose arrow, your path.
  • Green: the target, a good result.
  • Red: danger, no parking, brake lights.
  • Amber: indicators, amber lights, warnings.
  • Cyan: swept paths.
  • Blue: trucks and buses.

Fast on a phone

  • Blocks, buildings and markings are drawn once into 64 m tiles per detail level and reused.
  • Only cars, lights and overlays are redrawn each frame.
  • 30 fps cap, and no redraw when nothing moves, as now.
  • A spatial grid means collisions and sensors only check what's nearby, even on a big map.
  • Cars far from you only move along their lanes, with no physics until they get close.

Lessons

From first metres to a 16.5 m truck

Lessons get harder one step at a time. Each has three stars: no touches, within the time, and accurate (centred, straight, close to the kerb). It also counts shunts, the number of times you change direction. Later lessons repeat the same skills with harder cars.

  1. First metresForward, reverse, steering, stopping, in an empty car park.
  2. How a car turnsThe rear wheels cut inside and the front swings out. The swept paths are drawn while you drive.
  3. Into a bay, forwardsEmpty car park, wide bays.
  4. Into a bay between carsForwards, then the same bay in reverse.
  5. Parallel parking, big spaceAbout one and a half car lengths.
  6. Parallel parking, tight spaceDown to your car length plus about 1 m, California's handbook minimum.
  7. Leaving a tight spaceOut of a parallel space without touching the cars in front or behind.
  8. Angled bays and one-way aislesReading arrows and choosing the right way in.
  9. Your garageBay 561, nose in, arriving from the left, as today.

  11. Leavers and spot thievesWaiting for a space, and losing one if you hesitate.

The best part

Coaching worked out for your car

Driving handbooks teach reference points that are rules of thumb for an average car, and they disagree with each other. The simulation knows the exact geometry, so it can work out the moments for the car you are actually driving, and say now at the right time. The sketch above does it for the Atto 2 in its gap:

1

Full lock towards the kerb when the rear bumpers are level, about 0.8 m out (handbooks say 0.6–1 m).

2

Full lock the other way at about 48°, when your mirror is about 0.8 m past their rear bumper.

3

Straighten 0.25 m from the kerb (New York's test allows 30 cm), then roll forward to even the gaps.

A Smart and a Ram 1500 get very different numbers for the same gap, and the coach shows them as marks on the screen.

Tips the coach teaches

Taken from driving handbooks and test rules, checked on 4 October 2026. Handbooks disagree on several points. Where they do, the game says so and marks the moment for your car instead of quietly picking one rule. The UK parks on the left and the US and Germany on the right, so the coach says "towards the kerb", never "left" or "right".

Parallel parking

  • Gap: beginners want about 1.5 car lengths (DVSA, Oregon). The UK test uses about two. California's minimum is your length plus 0.9 m (3 ft).
  • Line up 0.6–1 m beside the car ahead (New York, Georgia), rear bumpers level or yours slightly ahead (California, DVSA).
  • Turn in when your rear wheels are level with its rear bumper. US handbooks use full lock; UK schools often teach one turn of the wheel (Bill Plant, Johnsons).
  • The 45° point: your centre pillar or front door reaches their rear bumper (Montana, Pennsylvania).
  • Finish close to the kerb: New York's test wants 30 cm (1 ft) or less and Georgia's allows 45 cm (18 in). The UK says "reasonably close" with no number.

Your mirror tip

It's in a real handbook

Georgia's driver manual: stop when the side mirror is in line with the other car's rear bumper, then steer the other way. Montana uses the kerb-side mirror the same way. Most others steer back later: the DVSA once your front is clear of the parked car, New York when your front wheels are opposite its back bumper, Oregon when your front tyre reaches its corner.

In the sketch, the Atto 2 at full lock should steer back when its mirror is about 0.8 m past their bumper. The coach can show the rule of thumb and the exact moment side by side.

Bay parking

  • Reverse in: drive past the bay, apply full lock when the bay's first line is level with the back seat in the rear side window, and straighten when the lines look parallel in both mirrors (learnerdriving.com, Johnsons).
  • Forwards in: from 1.5–2.4 m out, turn when your mirror reaches the bay's own near line (Georgia, Montana).
  • Why reverse in: you drive out forwards and can see, instead of reversing out half-blind. The Highway Code recommends it for driveways. Some German multi-storey car parks require forward parking (ADAC).
  • Angled bays: approach about 1 m out and turn sharply as your steering wheel passes the first line (Georgia).

Leaving a tight space

  • Back up close to the car behind, turn the wheels away from the kerb, signal, and check over both shoulders (New York).
  • Creep out while steering. Straighten when your front seat passes the bumper of the car ahead (Georgia).

Basics at walking pace

  • Reverse slowly. Look all around, and mainly through the rear window (Highway Code).
  • The front swings out as you turn. On long vehicles the rear wheels cut in towards the kerb (New Jersey CDL manual).
  • Tilt the kerb-side mirror down to see the kerb (ADAC, Bill Plant).
  • If you can't see, get out and look, or have someone guide you (Highway Code, StVO §9).
  • Turning the wheel while standing still wears the tyres and steering (DVSA), but the UK test doesn't mark it, so the game won't punish it.

Signals, hazards and who gets the space

  • UK: signalling gives you no priority for a space. Hazard lights are allowed only while stationary, to warn that you are temporarily blocking traffic. Never use them while driving or to excuse bad parking (Highway Code rule 116). By that rule, waiting for a space with hazards on can be fine, but reversing in with them on is not.
  • Germany: the space belongs to the first driver to reach it, even while they pull past to reverse in or wait for a car to leave (StVO §12(5)). Taking it anyway costs €10. Hazards are only for danger (§16), and ADAC treats them as misuse when double-parked.
  • US: it varies by state.

So the spot thief breaks the law in Germany and is only rude in the UK. The game needs a rule set per country.

Trailer

  • Hold the bottom of the steering wheel: the trailer goes the way your hand moves (California trailer handbook, BoatUS).
  • Use small inputs. Sharp corrections cause the jackknife, where the car and trailer fold.
  • Going wrong? Pull forward to straighten, then try again. Past 90°, stop and drive forward.

Truck at a dock

  • Back towards the driver's side, the side you can see, "even if it means going around the block" (Oregon CDL manual).
  • G.O.A.L.: Get Out And Look, and back slowly (OSHA).
  • Pull past the dock, parallel to it, then angle the trailer in (Ontario truck handbook).
  • With a spotter, agree the stop signal first, and stop whenever you lose sight of them (HSE).

The Smart, nose to the kerb

Legal in few places. Italy requires cars to park parallel to the kerb unless signs say otherwise (art. 157); a draft law would allow cars up to 2.70 m, but it was not in force when checked. Most US states require parallel parking within 30–45 cm of the curb. In the UK and Germany it is unclear and depends on the markings. So it would be a special level with a "check your local rules" note.

How the stars are judged

Real test rules: the UK fails a car parked outside its bay and faults one left at an angle or far from the kerb (DVSA examiner guidance). Pennsylvania allows one attempt and no more than three adjustments. Texas fails a wheel over the curb. The game turns these into numbers: inside the lines, within 30 cm of the kerb, and few shunts.

Notes before selling

  • One Pro unlock, not a subscription, is easier to sell for a training game. Bigger packs, like trucks, can come later. Avoid ads while driving, because they break concentration.
  • Paid content is just more data files plus a check that you own them. The engine is the same for everyone.
  • On the web: a payment provider such as Stripe or Paddle, with the unlock tied to an account or a licence key.
  • In the App Store and Google Play: unlocks inside the app go through their in-app purchase. Both have lower fees for small developers, so check their current terms.
  • Unlocks checked only on the phone can be cracked. That's fine at the start. Later the paid files can come from a server after a purchase check.
  • Car names: real brand names, logos and recognisable designs in a paid game usually need the car maker's permission. Many games use made-up names with real-world sizes instead. Each car file keeps name (what players see) separate from basedOn (the real model the numbers come from), so a store version can rename cars without touching the physics. Ask someone who knows trademark law before you sell. This is not legal advice.
